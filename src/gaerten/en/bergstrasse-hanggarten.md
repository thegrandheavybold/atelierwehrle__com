    This **hillside garden on the Bergstrasse** doesn’t sit beside the house. It leads you to it. The modern house, with its large glass façade, stands at the top of the slope, and everything below is designed as the approach. From a paved lane at the bottom, a gravel path climbs in gentle turns. Block steps and **natural stone walls** frame the route, and with every terrace the façade draws a little closer.

    The real question was how garden and architecture should talk to each other. Stone answers the clean lines of the house: walls and steps cut from massive blocks give the slope calm and permanence. The planting pushes back with life. It’s loose, naturalistic and layered in height. Tall, airy perennials such as Verbena bonariensis stand at the front, with cardoon and its silvery foliage behind them, along with grasses that catch the light. From inside the glazed façade, the garden reads as a deep, layered picture. Climb the path and you get the reverse. The house keeps appearing through flowers and stems, never as a freestanding block. Green-framed elements on the façade pick up the foliage, so house and garden share one colour family.

    Then there’s the speed of the **planting design**. The beds went in in May, and the photographs were taken in September. One season was enough. Verbena, cardoon and Japanese anemones turned the slope into a dense perennial garden, Mexican fleabane (Erigeron karvinskianus) spills along the foot of the walls, and thyme and sage hold the path edges together. Chard and strawberries grow among the perennials, where ornamental and edible planting overlap. By the stairs, a stone trough with a steel spout adds a quiet accent and draws in pollinators. In late summer, globe thistles stand in seed beside the last flowers, and the garden holds its structure as it turns towards autumn.

    On the Bergstrasse, topography, architecture and planting come together as one naturalistic whole. It’s a project that stands for precise landscape architecture and refined private gardens.
